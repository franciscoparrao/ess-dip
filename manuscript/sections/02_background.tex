\section{Background and related work}\label{sec:background}

\subsection{Selecting the number of clusters}\label{subsec:bg-k}

\revtext{Choosing the number of clusters $K$ is a classical problem in
unsupervised analysis, and a large family of \emph{internal} criteria address it by
scoring a partition against the geometry of the data alone.} \revtext{The gap
statistic \citep{Tibshirani2001} compares the within-cluster dispersion to its
expectation under a reference distribution with no clustering, typically
uniform over the data's bounding box. The average silhouette
\citep{Rousseeuw1987}, the Calinski--Harabasz ratio
\citep{CalinskiHarabasz1974} and the Davies--Bouldin index
\citep{DaviesBouldin1979} balance within- against between-cluster scatter.}
Comparative studies cover the many further variants
\citep{Arbelaitz2013}. \revtext{A second family selects $K$ through a
probabilistic model: model-based clustering fits a finite mixture and chooses
the number of components by an information criterion such as the BIC
\citep{GormleyMurphyRaftery2023}. That family has itself been extended to
spatially dependent data by embedding Gaussian processes in the mixture
\citep{Vanhatalo2021}.}

These criteria differ in how far they commit to a probability model,
and the distinction matters here. \revtext{The gap statistic and model-based
selection by BIC do rest on assumptions that treat observations as
independent. The gap compares the observed within-cluster dispersion with its
expectation under a reference distribution of independently scattered points,
and the mixture likelihood behind the BIC is a product over observations.}
\revtext{The average silhouette, the Calinski--Harabasz ratio and the
Davies--Bouldin index assume no probability model at all and are descriptive
summaries of a partition's geometry. On dependent data they are neither
correct nor incorrect, because they make no probabilistic claim that
dependence could violate.} \revtext{What all of them share is that dependence
between observations plays no role in their construction, so none of
them separates grouping produced by discrete classes from grouping that
autocorrelation alone produces.} The distinction
matters most for spatial data, because nearby
locations are correlated: ``everything is related to everything else, but
near things are more related than distant things'' \citep{Tobler1970}.

\subsection{Accounting for spatial dependence}\label{subsec:bg-spatial}

It is known across several fields that methods comparing data against
an independence reference report structure that the dependence alone can
account for. \revtext{In spatial epidemiology, \citet{Goovaerts2004}
show that a complete-spatial-randomness null over-identifies disease clusters.
In its place, they propose a geostatistical \emph{spatial neutral model},
whose realisations reproduce the histogram and variogram of the data by
sequential Gaussian simulation, and against this model far fewer locations remain
significant.}
In neuroimaging, \citet{Eklund2016} trace inflated false-positive rates in
cluster-extent inference to a mis-specified parametric model of the spatial
autocorrelation. \revtext{The common remedy is a null that \emph{preserves} the
spatial covariance structure while removing the effect being tested. It is
realised either by geostatistical simulation \citep{Goovaerts2004} or by
spatially constrained randomisation such as Moran spectral randomization
\citep{WagnerDray2015}.}

The same logic has been brought to bear on $K$ itself.
\revtext{\citet{HennigLin2015} calibrate a validity index (the gap, silhouette
or prediction strength) against its distribution under a problem-specific null
that encodes non-clustering structure, including spatial autocorrelation. They
use the calibrated index both to test for clustering and to estimate $K$.}
\revtext{On a biogeographic example, a plain Gaussian null favours eight
clusters, whereas the autocorrelation-aware null reduces this to two and renders the
apparent clustering non-significant.} \revtext{Their framework is the conceptual basis
of the present work, and its guiding principle is that all structure not
attributable to clustering belongs in the null model. The present method
instantiates exactly this principle for spatially autocorrelated continuous
fields, although the two contributions differ in how the calibration is obtained and in
the data setting they treat.} \revtext{First, \citet{HennigLin2015} calibrate
by simulation, so every decision requires an ensemble of null datasets. The
calibration developed here is instead analytic, through the effective sample
size, and this is what makes the near-linear full-scene cost of
Section~\ref{subsec:complexity} possible.} \revtext{Second, their construction
is formulated for discrete presence--absence data with an algorithmic
disjunction-parameter null. It addresses neither the \emph{continuous-field}
setting of gridded imagery nor the confounding of a smooth large-scale
\emph{trend} with discrete classes.} \revtext{The present contribution supplies
exactly these elements: a Gaussian-random-field null on the lattice,
a within-class range obtained by local range estimation, an adaptive
trend--residual separation, and an effective-sample-size calibration of a
modality statistic.} \revtext{Throughout the comparisons of
Sections~\ref{sec:simulation} and~\ref{sec:application}, the label
Hennig--Lin accordingly denotes their published null-model construction applied
to our setting, not this general framework, of which the
proposed method is itself an instance.}

\revtext{The quantitative device that makes this possible is the
\emph{effective sample size}: under spatial autocorrelation, the variance of a
sample statistic behaves as if computed from $\neff<n$ independent
observations. The reduction is governed by the integral of the autocorrelation
\citep{Cressie1993}, a quantity formalised as the \emph{integral range} in the
theory of ergodic random functions \citep{Lantuejoul1991,Lantuejoul2002}.}
\revtext{The concept recurs across statistics whenever dependent data carry
less information than their count suggests. \citet{Griffith2005} develops
effective geographic sample sizes for spatial sampling design, and
\citet{Berger2014} formalise the notion for general parametric inference.
\citet{VallejosOsorio2014} define and study it for spatial process models, and
\citet{Acosta2021} extend its computation to large spatial datasets through
block likelihoods.}
\revtext{\citet{Dutilleul1993} operationalised this idea to correct the degrees
of freedom of a correlation test between two spatial processes. We use it
instead to recalibrate, rather than to correct after the fact, the reference
distribution of a clustering criterion.} \revtext{The autocorrelation scale it
requires is the variogram range \citep{Matheron1963,Cressie1993}. The
safeguard against letting large homogeneous regions dominate its estimate is
\emph{declustering}, a standard geostatistical correction for spatially
clustered support \citep{IsaaksSrivastava1989,Deutsch1998}.}

\subsection{Modality as a clustering signal}\label{subsec:bg-modality}

Suppose one kept a dispersion-based index and improved only the
null, matching it to the covariance structure estimated from the data.
\revtext{The within-cluster dispersion that the index measures is a
variance-based quantity, and the contrast between classes contributes to the
very covariance that the null now reproduces. The null therefore generates
reference datasets of much the same dispersion as the data, whether or not
classes are present. The comparison then loses what it was meant to detect.}
\revtext{A criterion able to survive an
autocorrelation-matched null must read something the covariance does not
determine, and multimodality is the natural choice.} \revtext{The dip test of
\citet{HartiganHartigan1985} measures the departure of a univariate sample from
unimodality. Dip-means \citep{Kalogeratos2012} turns it into a divisive
procedure that splits a cluster only when a projection of it is significantly
multimodal, thereby estimating $K$.} \revtext{These methods assume independent
observations, and our contribution is to make the dip test valid under spatial
autocorrelation by calibrating it at the effective sample size. We also pair it
with the trend removal that prevents a smooth gradient from being read as two
modes.}

\subsection{Distinction from spatially constrained clustering}\label{subsec:bg-constrained}

A separate line of work \emph{imposes} spatial contiguity on the partition:
regionalisation methods such as SKATER \citep{Assuncao2006}, ClustGeo
\citep{Chavent2018} and the max-$p$-regions problem \citep{Duque2012} constrain
clusters to be spatially connected. \revtext{These methods address a different
question, namely how to partition space into contiguous regions, yet they still
require the number of regions as an input or a tuning parameter.} \revtext{We
do not constrain the partition but instead correct the criterion that selects
\emph{how many} groups are present.} \revtext{The two approaches are
complementary: the effective-sample-size calibration developed here could in
principle inform the stopping rule of a regionalisation, just as it informs the
recursion of Section~\ref{sec:method}.}
