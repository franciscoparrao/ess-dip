\section{Simulation study}\label{sec:simulation}

We assess the procedure on synthetic scenes with known ground truth, where the
number of classes, the strength of spatial autocorrelation, the presence of a
trend, and the class separability are all controlled. \revtext{The design
isolates the two failure modes of Section~\ref{subsec:null}, autocorrelation and
large-scale drift. It also measures how far the calibration keeps the recursion
from splitting: we count how often each method recovers a single class when the
scene contains exactly one.}

\subsection{Generative model}\label{subsec:design}

All scenes live on a $96\times96$ lattice with $p=6$ bands. The elementary
building block is a unit-variance Gaussian random field
$g_\sigma$, obtained by convolving Gaussian white noise with an isotropic
Gaussian kernel of bandwidth $\sigma$ (which sets the autocorrelation range)
and standardising. \revtext{Of the four world types that instantiate the
decomposition \eqref{eq:decomp}, the \emph{null} world ($K=1$) is pure
autocorrelation with no discrete structure: each band is an independent
$g_\sigma$, with $\sigma\in\{3,6,9\}$.} The \emph{trend} world ($K=1$) has smooth large-scale
variation but no classes: each band is $\theta\,t(s)+0.4\,g_3(s)$, where $t$
is a standardised planar--bilinear polynomial surface and the trend amplitude
is $\theta\in\{1.5,3.0\}$. \revtext{The \emph{structured} world
($K\in\{2,3,4,5\}$) embeds discrete classes in autocorrelated noise, with a
contiguous label map drawn as the pixelwise $\argmax$ of $K$ independent
fields $g_{\sigma_f}$, where $\sigma_f\in\{6,9\}$. Class $k$ is assigned a mean
spectrum $\bm\mu_k\sim\mathcal N(\bm 0,\delta^2 I_p)$ with separation
$\delta\in\{2,3,4\}$, and a per-band autocorrelated noise $g_2$ is added.}
\revtext{Finally, the \emph{mixed} world ($K\in\{3,4\}$) is the realistic case,
which combines classes \emph{and} drift: a structured scene ($\delta=3$, $\sigma_f=6$) plus a
global polynomial trend of amplitude $\theta\in\{1.5,3.0\}$.}
\revtext{The factorial of these settings yields $33$ cells ($3$ null, $2$ trend,
$24$ structured, $4$ mixed), each realised $15$ times with independent
seeds, for $495$ scenes in total.}

\subsection{Methods and metrics}\label{subsec:methods}

\revtext{We compare sixteen criteria, and the decision rule of each is
summarised here. Section~S2 of the supplementary material specifies every one
in full. It gives all tuning constants, the subsampling used by the indices
that require pairwise distances, and the way the multiple-testing correction is
applied. The released code reproduces each number with its seeds.}
\revtext{Four are standard internal indices evaluated over $K=1,\dots,8$ on
MiniBatch $k$-means partitions. The first is the gap statistic
\citep{Tibshirani2001}, with eight uniform reference datasets over the bounding
box and the one-standard-error rule. The others are the average silhouette,
Calinski--Harabasz and Davies--Bouldin, each maximised over $K$ except
Davies--Bouldin, which is minimised.} These four are the
original formulations, with no null model added by us. \revtext{The
null-calibrated criteria in this benchmark are the autocorrelation-matched gap
described next, the Hennig--Lin variants, and the proposed family. In each case
the null was introduced by the work that proposed that calibration, not by the
papers that introduced the indices themselves.} A fifth criterion is the most
direct spatial adaptation of an existing index, the \emph{gap with an
autocorrelated reference}. \revtext{It is identical to the gap statistic, with
the same one-standard-error rule, except in its eight reference datasets. These
are drawn not uniformly over the bounding box but as per-band Gaussian random
fields whose $1/e$ range matches the tile-median estimate of
Section~\ref{subsec:range}.} The reference fields are scaled to each band's
standard deviation as estimated from the scene itself, since no
deployable version of the criterion has access to the true value. This
criterion isolates how far
correcting only the reference's autocorrelation goes. \revtext{The silhouette,
Calinski--Harabasz and Davies--Bouldin indices cannot return $K=1$ by
construction; we return to this asymmetry below.} \revtext{Two more are the
calibrated-validity-index method of \citet{HennigLin2015}, the procedure
ESS-Dip extends, which calibrates the average silhouette width by parametric
bootstrap against a null model. We run it with an \emph{i.i.d.} Gaussian null,
the autocorrelation-blind application, and with a \emph{spatial} null. The
spatial null consists of phase-randomised surrogates preserving each band's
variogram, their autocorrelation-aware variant adapted to the gridded
setting.} The
calibrated index is
$V(K)=\bigl(\mathrm{ASW}(K)-\overline{\mathrm{ASW}}_{\mathrm{null}}(K)\bigr)/
\mathrm{sd}_{\mathrm{null}}(K)$, computed from $B=20$ null datasets (scenes
subsampled to $1200$ pixels for tractability); $\hat K$ is the maximiser of
$V$ when $\max_K V(K)>1.96$ and $\hat K=1$ otherwise. \revtext{An eighth
baseline, plain \emph{dip-means}, applies the same modality test at the nominal
sample size. It omits the effective-sample-size calibration, the local range and
the trend removal, and so isolates the contribution of that spatial machinery.}
\revtext{The ninth is the proposed method of Section~\ref{sec:method}, denoted
\textbf{ESS-Dip}: Algorithm~\ref{alg:essdip} with the local within-class
range \eqref{eq:areamed}, adaptive trend removal \eqref{eq:detrendrule} and a
$B=5$ ensemble. We fix $\alpha=0.05$, $\tau=0.25$, tile side $t=24$ and
$\nu=12$ throughout, with no per-scene tuning.} \revtext{The tenth,
\textbf{ESS-Dip-R}, is its recall-oriented variant, which calibrates the dip
against the Gaussian null of Assumption~\ref{ass:null} rather than the uniform
least-favourable null. This reference is less conservative and recovers more
structure at the same specificity (Section~\ref{subsec:simresults}).}
\revtext{The next four are ablations of ESS-Dip, each disabling one component
to attribute the contribution of every ingredient. \emph{Global range} estimates
the $\ell^2$ range over the whole image rather than as a tile median, \emph{no
trend removal} sets $\tau=\infty$, and \emph{forced trend removal} sets
$\tau=0$. \emph{Declustering} is the precursor that subsamples the scene to one
pixel per decorrelation patch and applies the dip test to the thinned,
approximately independent sample.} \revtext{The fifteenth, \emph{SGS-Dip},
replaces the per-node effective-sample-size calibration by the recursive
Gaussian-simulation reference \eqref{eq:sgstest} of Section~\ref{subsec:sgs},
with $B=40$ simulations per node, and the sixteenth adds a Bonferroni
multiple-testing correction to it.}

Three metrics summarise performance. \emph{Specificity} is the fraction of
\revtext{null and trend scenes (the $75$ scenes with $K=1$) for which $\hat K=1$,
the empirical analogue of Proposition~\ref{prop:fpr}.} \emph{Exact-$K$ accuracy}
is the fraction of scenes with $\hat K=K_{\text{true}}$, reported separately
for the structured and mixed worlds, with the mean absolute error
$\mathrm{MAE}=\E\,\lvert\hat K-K_{\text{true}}\rvert$. \revtext{The
\emph{balanced score}, the mean of specificity, structured accuracy and mixed
accuracy, is a single figure that rewards methods that are strong across all
three regimes rather than in one.}

\paragraph{Standard indices report classes on every class-free scene.}
On the $75$ class-free scenes, every standard index reports spurious structure:
the $\hat K{=}1$ rate is $0.00$ for all four. The failure is severe, not
marginal: the gap statistic returns, on average, $\hat K=7.7$ classes on
fields generated without any, and the silhouette, Calinski--Harabasz and
Davies--Bouldin indices average $2.9$, $3.6$ and $4.1$, respectively. This is the
over-detection anticipated in Section~\ref{subsec:null}, now quantified: under
spatial autocorrelation, an independence null cannot recognise the absence of
discrete structure in the sense of \eqref{eq:decomp}. \revtext{Nor is the failure
repaired by the simplest spatial correction: the gap with an
autocorrelation-matched reference raises specificity only to $0.21$. This is because
matching the reference's autocorrelation addresses one of the two failure modes
but not the other, the smooth drift.} \revtext{On trend scenes, its reference fields
carry no drift, and the criterion reads the gradient as structure
($\hat K\approx7$ on average). In the mixed regime, its accuracy collapses to
$0.10$, and its balanced score, $0.427$, lies far below that of the proposed
family.} Fixing the reference is not enough: both the
dispersion statistic itself and the trend must be addressed.

\paragraph{The proposed family leaves the class-free scenes undivided.}
Every member of the proposed family (ESS-Dip and all four ablations)
attains specificity $1.00$: in $75$ out of $75$ class-free scenes, it returns a
single class. \revtext{This is the empirical content of
Proposition~\ref{prop:fpr}, and it holds whether the class-free scene is stationary
(null) or carries a smooth drift (trend). This confirms that the effective-sample-size
calibration and the adaptive detrending together neutralise both failure modes.}

\paragraph{Specificity does not cost competitiveness.}
On the structured scenes, the gap statistic is the most accurate
($0.97$): with well-separated classes and an aligned null, it is hard to beat,
and we do not beat it. \revtext{ESS-Dip reaches $0.81$, ahead of the silhouette ($0.76$) and
Davies--Bouldin ($0.70$) and close to Calinski--Harabasz ($0.85$). It is also
the \emph{only} one of these methods with non-zero specificity.} On the realistic mixed
scenes, ESS-Dip reaches $0.50$, ahead of every standard index ($0.40$ for the
best), because the adaptive detrending removes the drift that causes them to collapse.
\revtext{The recall variant ESS-Dip-R, which calibrates against
the Gaussian null of Assumption~\ref{ass:null} rather than the conservative
uniform one, does better still. At the same unit specificity, it raises structured accuracy to
$0.89$ and mixed accuracy to $0.72$, for a balanced score of $0.868$, the best of
any method.}
ESS-Dip itself scores $0.771$, already far above the next non-proposed method
($0.638$) and more than twice the best standard index ($0.418$). These synthetic
fields are Gaussian, so the model-matched ESS-Dip-R is favoured here; its
specificity and recall gains carry over to the real scenes of
Section~\ref{sec:application}. Standard indices that look strong on pure
structured scenes collapse once specificity is taken into account.

\paragraph{Comparison with the method that ESS-Dip extends.}
The calibrated-validity-index framework of \citet{HennigLin2015} is
instructive precisely because the present method generalises it. Applied with
an independence (i.i.d.) null, it inherits the over-detection of the standard
indices (specificity $0.00$, balanced score $0.280$), confirming that the
framework alone, without a spatial null, does not address the problem. With its
autocorrelation-aware (spatial) null, it recovers much of the lost specificity
($0.80$) and a comparable structured accuracy ($0.72$), a clear vindication of
its central idea. Two differences remain, and they are the contribution of this
paper. First, its specificity, at $0.80$, still admits one class-free scene in
five as clustered, whereas the within-class calibration of ESS-Dip admits none.
\revtext{Second, and decisively, its accuracy on the mixed regime is only
$0.17$, no better than the i.i.d.\ variant and far below ESS-Dip's
$0.50$. This is because a phase-randomised null preserves the trend along with
the autocorrelation, and so cannot separate a smooth drift from discrete classes.} The explicit
trend--residual decomposition of Section~\ref{subsec:detrend}, absent from the
calibrated-index framework, is what closes this gap. \revtext{This comparison is
insensitive to the significance threshold: the value used, $V>1.96$ (the
one-sided $2.5\%$ level for the calibrated index under approximate normality),
and the alternatives $V>1.64$ and $V>2.33$ all leave the conclusion intact.}
At every threshold, the i.i.d.\ null fails on specificity ($0.00$), and the spatial
null controls specificity ($0.77$--$0.83$) but not the mixed regime
($\le0.08$). Figure~\ref{fig:recover} makes the mixed-scene
behaviour concrete on one realisation: the gap statistic and the Hennig--Lin
null fragment the scene into six and eight clusters, respectively, whereas ESS-Dip recovers
the three true classes.

\subsection{Ablation}\label{subsec:ablation}

The ablations isolate where ESS-Dip's accuracy comes from
(Table~\ref{tab:bench}, lower block). The single largest contribution is the
\emph{local within-class range} of Section~\ref{subsec:range}. \revtext{Replacing
it with a scene-wide range lowers structured accuracy from $0.81$ to $0.63$ and
mixed accuracy from $0.50$ to $0.28$, while leaving specificity untouched.} This confirms the diagnosis of
Section~\ref{subsec:range}: a global range conflates the between-class
mosaic with the within-class scale, inflating $\ell$, collapsing $\neff$, and
starving the test of power.
\revtext{\emph{Adaptive} trend removal matters in both directions: forcing
detrending on every scene ($\tau=0$) destroys structured accuracy ($0.19$),
because the polynomial absorbs genuine between-class signal when no trend is
present. Disabling it ($\tau=\infty$) reduces mixed accuracy to $0.13$, because
an unremoved drift inflates the range.} Removing the drift \emph{only when present}
preserves both ($0.81$ structured, $0.50$ mixed). \revtext{The declustering
precursor discards all but one pixel per patch. It matches the others on
specificity but trails on accuracy ($0.08$ mixed), which confirms the value of
calibrating on the full pixel set rather than thinning it.} \revtext{At the
opposite extreme, plain dip-means (Table~\ref{tab:bench}) removes the spatial
apparatus entirely and applies the dip at the nominal size. The modality signal
alone recovers structure well ($0.92$, second only to the gap statistic).
Without the effective-sample-size calibration, however, its specificity falls to
$0.52$, and without trend removal its mixed accuracy collapses to $0.02$.}
\revtext{The contribution of ESS-Dip is therefore not the modality test, which
is already strong, but the spatial calibration and trend separation, which
turn a high-recall, low-specificity splitter into a precision-controlled
homogeneity test. The cost is a modest loss of structured recall ($0.92$ to
$0.81$).}

\subsection{Geostatistical reference and the effective-sample-size
approximation}\label{subsec:geostat-ref}

Section~\ref{subsec:range} offers, alongside the default $\ell^{2}$
proxy, a fitted variogram and its integral range, and
Section~\ref{subsec:sgs} offers the Gaussian-simulation reference
\eqref{eq:sgstest} in place of the effective-sample-size shortcut. \revtext{Section~S4
of the supplementary material compares all three and reports two findings used
elsewhere in this paper.} \revtext{First, as a single homogeneity test on a full
rectangular domain, the simulation reference decides correctly on every world.
It accepts homogeneity on the class-free ones ($p=0.35$, $0.46$) and rejects it
where classes are present ($p<10^{-3}$). This is the assumption-light check
referred to in Section~\ref{subsec:prop}.} \revtext{The mean-based integral
range, however, is some three times larger than $\ell^{2}$ on these fields and
so loses recall, which is why the proxy is the default.} \revtext{Second, promoting the
simulation reference to a recursive estimator does not pay: without a
multiple-testing correction, it over-splits (specificity $0.85$, balanced score
$0.535$). With a Bonferroni correction, it becomes competitive on controlled
fields (balanced score $0.710$ against ESS-Dip's $0.771$). On real imagery,
however, its per-node variogram, fitted on irregular non-rectangular supports,
leaves it behind (Section~\ref{subsec:realspec}).} Moreover, it requires $M$ simulations per node, against the
cached near-linear cost of \eqref{eq:test}. The apparent paradox that a
conservative correction raises accuracy is resolved in Section~S4: the
correction lowers $\hat K$ in every world, and accuracy rises because the
uncorrected recursion overshoots.

\subsection{Computational performance}\label{subsec:timing}

Figure~\ref{fig:timing} reports single-threaded wall-clock time on structured
scenes. \revtext{Across image sizes from $\sim\!2{,}300$ to $\sim\!66{,}000$
pixels, ESS-Dip scales with an empirical exponent
$\mathrm{d}\log t/\mathrm{d}\log N = 0.86$ (panel~a), a near-linear rate
consistent with Section~\ref{subsec:complexity}. ESS-Dip is also a constant
factor (about $1.2$--$2\times$) faster than the gap statistic, which scales
similarly but carries the overhead of its reference replicates.} Cost grows sub-linearly with
the band count $p$ (panel~b: from $0.31$\,s at $p=3$ to $1.02$\,s at $p=40$, at
$N=128^2$). Peak memory for a $256\times256\times6$ cube is $8.9$\,MB, about three times
the input cube, so the method is memory-light at scene scale. \revtext{The exact
Gaussian-simulation homogeneity test \eqref{eq:sgstest} with $B=120$ costs
$0.10$--$0.34$\,s over the same sizes, comparable to a single
ESS-Dip evaluation because it concerns a single node. A fully recursive
simulation null would multiply this cost by the number of nodes, and that is the
cost the effective-sample-size calibration is designed to avoid.}

\subsection{Robustness to anisotropy}\label{subsec:aniso}

The decorrelation area \eqref{eq:areamed} is estimated from a radially averaged
(isotropic) variogram, yet directional autocorrelation does not degrade the
method. \revtext{We tested null and structured worlds whose within-class range
differs by a factor of up to eight between the two axes. At every anisotropy
ratio tested ($1,2,4,8$), ESS-Dip retains specificity $1.00$ and structured
accuracy $1.00$. On the same class-free fields, the gap statistic's specificity
falls from $0.88$ to $0.12$ as the field elongates.} \revtext{The isotropic
scalar is thus an adequate summary even under marked anisotropy, because the
modality signal it feeds is direction-agnostic. A fully anisotropic variogram
(Section~\ref{sec:discussion}) is a refinement rather than a necessity.}

\subsection{Robustness to misestimation of the range}\label{subsec:misspec}

The calibration consumes a single estimated scale, so the practical question
is how much error in that scale the test tolerates. \revtext{We answer it
directly: the locally estimated range $\ell$ is multiplied by a factor
$f\in\{\tfrac14,\tfrac12,\tfrac34,1,\tfrac32,2,4\}$ (the decorrelation
area scales as $f^2$). The full pipeline is then re-run with the perturbed value,
with $15$ paired scenes each, on the null world (specificity) and on the
structured world with $K=4$ (recovery).} Table~\ref{tab:misspec} reports the
exact values, and Figure~\ref{fig:misspec} the response curves. \revtext{The
level is essentially indifferent to the error: overestimating the range only
makes the test more conservative. Even a four-fold \emph{under}estimate, which
inflates the effective sample size sixteen-fold, raises the false-split rate
merely to $0.07$, near the nominal $\alpha$, because the least-favourable
uniform calibration absorbs most of the slack (Remark~\ref{rem:prop}).} \revtext{Recovery is
asymmetric in the opposite direction: it survives underestimation
unharmed (full recovery at $f=\tfrac14$) and degrades only under overestimation.
It retains $\hat K=4$ in $73\%$ of scenes at $f=\tfrac32$ and collapses to no
splits at $f=4$, where the effective sample size falls below the testable
minimum.} The deployed estimator therefore lies on the benign side of both
asymmetries. \revtext{Errors of up to a factor of two in the range move the
operating point along the precision--recall curve rather than breaking the
calibration. The failure mode of a gross overestimate is silence, not
invention.}

\subsection{Class imbalance}\label{subsec:imbalance}

The Sentinel-2 result (Section~\ref{subsec:realfull}) suggests that a dominant
class hides minorities; here we quantify how severe the imbalance must be.
\revtext{Two-class scenes are generated with a contiguous minority class
occupying a controlled proportion $\pi\in\{0.50,0.30,0.20,0.10,0.05,0.02\}$ of
the domain. The minority is a Gaussian random field thresholded at its
$(1-\pi)$ quantile, and the class means are drawn as in
Section~\ref{subsec:design}. There are $15$ replicates at each of two
separations ($\mathrm{sep}=3,4$), which are pooled, and the same scene is shown
to every method.} Table~\ref{tab:imbalance} reports the minority-detection rate
$\Prob(\hat K\ge2)$ and the mean $\hat K$; Figure~\ref{fig:imbalance}
displays the degradation curves. \revtext{ESS-Dip detects the minority
reliably down to $\pi=0.20$ ($0.90$), degrades at $\pi=0.10$ ($0.73$), and is
blind from $\pi=0.05$ downwards. The recall variant ESS-Dip-R extends the
reliable regime to $\pi=0.10$ ($0.93$) and detects half the cases at
$\pi=0.05$.} \revtext{The mechanism is the calibration itself: a minority
occupying proportion $\pi$ contributes a mode of mass $\pi$ to the projected
distribution. Once $\pi\,\neff$ falls to the order of the decorrelation area,
the excess mass is indistinguishable from autocorrelated fluctuation.}
\revtext{The apparent robustness of the baselines at extreme imbalance is not
creditable as detection: their false-split rate on class-free scenes is near
one (Table~\ref{tab:bench}), so a reported $\hat K\ge2$ carries no evidence. At
$\pi=0.02$, moreover, the silhouette returns $\hat K\approx3$ by fragmenting the
majority class rather than isolating the minority.} \revtext{As practical guidance, minorities below about
a fifth of the scene call for ESS-Dip-R, and minorities below a twentieth are beyond
the specificity-controlled family at this separation, a limitation
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} returns to.}
