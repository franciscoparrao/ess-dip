\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

\revtext{Unsupervised classification partitions a data set into groups without
labelled examples, and in doing so it must answer a prior question: how many
groups are there?} \revtext{The choice of the number of clusters $K$ governs
every downstream interpretation. In the analysis of remotely sensed imagery,
$K$ is the number of land-cover classes an analyst will map, and the choice is
made routinely and consequentially.} \revtext{A mature toolkit exists to make
it. The gap statistic \citep{Tibshirani2001}, the average silhouette
\citep{Rousseeuw1987}, the Calinski--Harabasz \citep{CalinskiHarabasz1974}
and Davies--Bouldin \citep{DaviesBouldin1979} indices each score a partition by
its internal geometry and select the $K$ that optimises the score.}
The stakes are concrete. \revtext{An analyst mapping land cover from a
satellite scene must commit to $K$ for the large family of methods that take
it as input, among them $k$-means and finite mixtures. Methods that instead
determine the number of groups on the fly, such as DBSCAN \citep{Ester1996},
replace that choice by tuning parameters that govern it.} Either way, every
downstream product (class areas, change statistics, sampling strata, training
masks) inherits the decision. \revtext{If $K$ is set too high, single crop fields
fragment into spurious sub-classes that propagate into those products, and if it is set
too low, real classes merge.} \revtext{The same prior decision arises when
delineating geochemical domains, zoning a mineral deposit, or partitioning a
scene into regions for stratified field sampling. In each case, the analyst
must first ask how many classes the data actually support.}

These criteria share a blind spot: none of them takes the dependence
between observations into account. \revtext{The gap statistic makes the
omission explicit, in a reference distribution of points scattered
independently across the data's range. The silhouette, Calinski--Harabasz and
Davies--Bouldin indices carry no reference distribution at all and score
geometry in a way that is silent about how the points came to be placed where
they are.} \revtext{Spatial data are not exchangeable:
neighbouring pixels are autocorrelated \citep{Tobler1970}, so the
effective number of independent observations is far below the nominal pixel
count \citep{Cressie1993}. In addition, a smooth illumination or topographic
gradient spreads the feature cloud along a manifold that an independence null
cannot distinguish from genuine groups.} \revtext{The consequence, which we
quantify in Sections~\ref{sec:simulation}--\ref{sec:application}, is
systematic \emph{over-detection}. Throughout, the partitions that the criteria
score are produced by $k$-means, the dominant choice in this setting.
Statements about a criterion's behaviour are therefore statements about the
criterion--$k$-means pair, a dependence that Section~\ref{subsec:methods}
makes explicit.} \revtext{On synthetic fields containing no discrete classes,
the gap statistic reports, on average, between seven and eight classes. On
real imagery, the silhouette, Calinski--Harabasz and Davies--Bouldin indices
fragment a single, homogeneous crop field into several sub-classes in every
instance we examined.}

\revtext{None of this makes those criteria wrong. A clustering is not a fact
waiting to be uncovered: which groupings count as clusters depends on what the
analysis is for \citep{Hennig2015}. An analyst may have good reason to
partition an autocorrelated field into contiguous units, whether or not the
partition could be explained by the autocorrelation alone.} \revtext{What these
criteria cannot do is answer one particular question, the question this paper
addresses: does a region contain discrete classes, over and above the variation
that a single autocorrelated class already produces?} \revtext{That question is
the relevant one whenever the classes are meant to denote distinct ground
objects rather than convenient subdivisions. Against it, a criterion that
reports several classes on a field simulated with one is uninformative.}

Answering it is not a matter of swapping in a better null alone. \revtext{The
within-cluster dispersion that these criteria minimise is a variance-based
quantity. A reference matched to the data's spatial covariance structure, its
variogram, therefore reproduces the same dispersion whether or not classes are
present. This happens because the between-class contrast is itself part of the
covariance that the reference is matched to. The comparison then has nothing
left to detect.} \revtext{The null must change, but so must the statistic, for two reasons.
Distinguishing classes from autocorrelation requires information beyond the
covariance, namely the \emph{multimodality} of the feature distribution.
Distinguishing real classes from a smooth trend requires separating a
continuous drift from discrete structure, a separation that the geostatistical
decomposition into trend and residual makes natural.} \revtext{The idea of
calibrating a clustering criterion against an autocorrelation-aware null is
itself not new: \citet{HennigLin2015} do exactly this for discrete
presence--absence data. However, the idea has not been carried to the
continuous-field setting of gridded imagery, nor has the confounding of trend
with class been addressed in selecting $K$.}

\revtext{This paper supplies both: we recast the question \emph{how many
classes?} as a sequence of homogeneity decisions. At each node, we test whether
a region is a single class or instead carries \emph{multimodality in excess of
spatial autocorrelation}. We make that test valid on autocorrelated fields by
calibrating it at the effective sample size.} The
construction rests on three geostatistical devices. \revtext{The first is the
effective sample size itself \citep{Cressie1993,Dutilleul1993}, which sets the
calibration. It is evaluated at the indicator integral range appropriate to a
functional of the distribution, not at the field integral range that governs
the mean (Section~\ref{subsec:sgs}).} The second is a \emph{local} estimate of
the within-class autocorrelation range, in the manner of declustering
\citep{IsaaksSrivastava1989,Deutsch1998}, which prevents the between-class
mosaic from corrupting that calibration. The third is an adaptive
trend--residual decomposition, which removes a smooth drift only when it is
statistically resolvable. A recursive partitioning driven by the calibrated dip statistic
\citep{HartiganHartigan1985} returns $K$. \revtext{Because the test is tuned
for specificity, it seldom divides a region that the null can account for. As
with any test, failing to reject is not evidence that a region is homogeneous,
only that the data do not contradict homogeneity at the effective
sample size.} That asymmetry makes ESS-Dip as useful as a homogeneity guard or
a stopping rule for region-growing as it is as a conservative estimator of $K$.

The paper makes four contributions. First, we document, on
controlled ground truth, that the standard number-of-clusters criteria report
more than one class on every one of the $75$ scenes we simulate without
discrete classes (Section~\ref{sec:simulation}). \revtext{Second, we propose
\emph{ESS-Dip}, an effective-sample-size-calibrated test of cluster
homogeneity, that is, of whether a region holds discrete structure beyond its
spatial autocorrelation. The test combines a within-class range from local
range estimation with adaptive trend removal (Section~\ref{sec:method}), and, applied recursively, it returns a conservative $K$.} \revtext{For a stylised
version of the test, we prove that the probability of dividing a homogeneous
region vanishes (Proposition~\ref{prop:fpr}), whereas the deployed procedure is
assessed empirically.}
A recall-oriented variant, ESS-Dip-R, trades the distribution-free uniform
null for the model's Gaussian null to recover more structure at the same
specificity. Third, we evaluate sixteen criteria on a factorial simulation
with known $K$. They include the most direct spatial adaptation of a standard
index (the gap statistic with an autocorrelation-matched reference) and the
calibrated-validity-index method of \citet{HennigLin2015} that ESS-Dip
generalises. \revtext{ESS-Dip reaches unit specificity, and its recall variant
ESS-Dip-R attains the best balanced score at the same specificity. An ablation
attributes the gain to the local range and the adaptive detrending
(Section~\ref{sec:simulation}).} \revtext{Fourth, we confirm this behaviour on
three real scenes spanning hyperspectral and multispectral sensing: Indian
Pines, Salinas, and a Sentinel-2 scene labelled by ESA WorldCover. On these
scenes, ESS-Dip identifies single land-cover regions as homogeneous where the
standard criteria do not (Section~\ref{sec:application}).}

The remainder of the paper is organised as follows.
Section~\ref{sec:background} reviews number-of-clusters criteria, the
geostatistical notions of effective sample size and declustering, and
modality-based clustering, and positions the contribution.
\revtext{Section~\ref{sec:method} develops the method and its calibration, and
Sections~\ref{sec:simulation} and~\ref{sec:application} report the simulation
study and the application to land-cover imagery.} Section~\ref{sec:discussion}
discusses the precision--recall trade-off and limitations, and
Section~\ref{sec:conclusions} concludes. Code and data are released to
reproduce every result.
