homogeneity and the number of classes in spatially autocorrelated data}
\author{F. Parra \and [co-authors]}

\begin{abstract}
Selecting the number of clusters $K$ is a basic step in unsupervised
classification. The standard internal criteria (the gap statistic, the
average silhouette, the Calinski--Harabasz and Davies--Bouldin indices)
take no account of the dependence between observations, so on spatial
data they leave one question unanswered: whether an apparent grouping reflects
discrete classes, or is already produced by the autocorrelation of a single
class. Neighbouring locations are correlated, so a scene carries far less
information than its pixel count suggests, and a smooth large-scale trend
mimics discrete structure. The effect is systematic: on autocorrelated fields
simulated without discrete classes these criteria report more than one class in
every one of $75$ realisations, and on real imagery they divide single
land-cover regions. We recast the choice of $K$ as a recursive homogeneity test: at each step,
whether a region is a single class or carries \emph{multimodality in excess of
spatial autocorrelation}. The proposed method, ESS-Dip, evaluates the Hartigan dip
statistic along the locally optimal split direction. It calibrates the statistic
against the effective sample size derived from a within-class autocorrelation
range, which a local declustering estimator separates from the between-class
scale; a low-order trend is removed adaptively. A recursive partitioning returns $K$,
and for a stylised version of the test we give conditions under which
the probability of dividing a homogeneous region vanishes. On a factorial
simulation with known ground truth, ESS-Dip reaches unit specificity with
competitive recovery; its recall-oriented variant ESS-Dip-R, calibrated against
the model's Gaussian null, attains the best balanced score of sixteen criteria
at the same specificity. On the Indian Pines, Salinas and Sentinel-2 scenes both
identify single land-cover regions as homogeneous where the standard criteria
do not. The method is conservative by design: it seldom divides a region the
null can account for, at the cost of power against weakly separated or strongly
imbalanced classes. That trade-off, which we characterise, suits it to use as a
homogeneity guard or a stopping rule rather than as an exhaustive enumerator of
classes. Code and data are released.
\end{abstract}
