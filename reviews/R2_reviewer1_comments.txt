REVIEWER #1
The paper is improved and I'm largely fine with the method and the research,
however the presentation is still not very reader friendly by often not
explaining things precisely enough, sometimes misleading wording, and an
occasionally confusing use of terminology, assuming that the reader knows the
author's use which cannot be taken for granted, even though all this has
somewhat improved (which raised a few new issues though). I still think a web
supplement clarifying details and potentially outsourcing some material that
currently is in the paper would help as the paper is too long. The added
Appendix unfortunately says surprisingly little beyond what is already said in
the main text. Furthermore, some claims regarding existing methodology are
still misleading. Furthermore, the authors still promise more in places than
the method can deliver.

[G1] General: The paper implicitly suggests in many places that there is a
uniquely true clustering of a dataset. This is not the case, see Hennig (2015).
In particular, a data analyst may in some situations be interested in
clustering data regardless of whether some of the clustering can be explained
by spatial autocorrelation, which may occasionally be useful (for example for
avalanche risk mapping and the like). Therefore it is not in general true that
structure that can be explained by spatial autocorrelation should not be
clustered. It is for sure relevant what the authors do here because in many
applications indeed we want to tell apart clustering that has meaning beyond
the spatial autocorrelation and clustering that may be explained by the spatial
structure alone. However, any wording that suggests that clusterings can only
be valid if spatial autocorrelation is taken into account should be changed
(starting from the abstract "miscalibrated on spatial data"). Indexes like the
Silhouette index may well be fine for spatial data if the application doesn't
require what is required here. Also, e.g. p. 2, l.40/41 "A criterion that
cannot recognise the absence of clusters is a precarious foundation for
deciding their number." The "absence of clusters" is not an absolute fact but
relative to your definition what counts as a cluster. There are more instances
of this kind.

[G2] General: I don't think the authors have commented on the possibility that
2-means may cut through a cluster in situations with more than 2 clusters in an
early stage of the algorithm.

[1] p. 2, l.16/17 "An analyst mapping land cover from a satellite scene must
fix K before any unsupervised classifier runs" - not true in general. There are
clustering methods (such as DBSCAN) that estimate K on the fly.

[2] l.42-45 "Correcting this is not a matter of swapping in a better null."
Isn't this what you are doing (at least in some sense)? I don't really see
what's wrong with "swapping in a better null".

[3] Terms like "scene", "mosaic", "MiniBatch" are used without explicit
introduction (it is always good to link terminology explicitly to your model
and problem, and generally it is good to use always the same term for the same
thing. Later you use terms like "\ell^2 proxy", "1/e proxy" again without
having explicitly defined what you mean. The reader can guess it in most cases
but will lose time asking themselves "what was that?", potentially looking
around on the paper without success. Explain all terminology explicitly and
then don't switch it.

[4] p. 3, l.20: "its negatives are trustworthy: when it declares a region
homogeneous that verdict can be relied on". Not so. In general if a statistical
test doesn't reject a null hypothesis, it never means that the null hypothesis
is true, Obviously there can be deviations from homogeneity that are so weak
that the available data can't find them. Furthermore your test is mostly
conservative, meaning that absolutely there are instances where the null
hypothesis is not true and the test will not reject (whether you tried such
instances in your simulation or not). This is not a specific problem with your
method; in general homogeneity and weak non-homogeneity can often not be told
apart, but still, don't write as if it were different.

[5] l.32: As Proposition 1 is "stylised", it doesn't guarantee anything for the
proposed method. There is no such thing as a proven "asymptotic
false-split-control property" for the method as a whole. It's all heuristic
arguments.

[6] p. 4, l.24/25: "None of these criteria was proposed with a distribution
theory that requires independent observations." Well, some yes (mixture/BIC,
gap), some no (ASW, DB). Be precise.

[7] By the way, there is also mixture model-based clustering taking spatial
autocorrelation into account, see, e.g., Vanhatalo et al. (2021).

[8] l.37: "That an independence reference over-detects structure in dependent
data is known across several fields." Be precise. Structure is detected by
methods for detecting structure. A reference is not a method but a model.

[9] p. 5, l.46/47: "Because k-means dispersion is itself a variance-based
quantity, a null matched to the data's covariance structure can explain away
the very clusters one seeks" - I don't understand the logic. k-means dispersion
is one thing, the null is another. Maybe what the authors have in mind makes
sense but is underexplained here.

[10] p. 7/8 "Internal validity criteria for K, namely the gap statistic
(Tibshirani et al. 2001), the average silhouette, Calinski-Harabasz and
Davies-Bouldin, compare a within-cluster dispersion against the value expected
with no clustering, under an independent reference distribution." This is true
for the gap statistic but not for the other criteria listed here.

[11] p. 8, l. 3: "autocorrelation reduces the number of independent
observations far below N" - as a standard there aren't any independent
observations here (you mean what is called "effective sample size", which is
not a number of actually independent observations).

[12] "Second, a smooth drift \mu spreads the feature cloud along a
low-dimensional manifold and is read as structure by an independence null." You
could have an independence null that takes drift into account but not
dependence.

[13] l.14 "null H_0"

[28] Sec. 3.8: Regarding the k-means initialisation variability I think what is
done here is not the best that could be done. k-means is about optimising the
k-means objective function, so if you get B solutions from B initialisations,
some of these are good and some aren't. You shouldn't just use the median or
mean of K derived from these. The way I think it should be done is that you run
B initialisations for each K and then for each K you use the best solution
according to the objective function (this is what Python Kmeans actually does
when setting n_init).

[29] p.12, l.39: "The calibration (6) is designed so that, in the absence of
discrete structure, the recursion does not split." There is no guarantee that
this is so, yet you write as if there were one.

[30] Sec. 3.9: "The result is deliberately stylised" - this is sugarcoating the
fact that you apparently don't have a better one (if you had one it should be
there). It is fine to present this result, but be honest about its limitations
not only in this place but also whenever you make reference to it. There is no
"guarantee" of anything here regarding actual application of your method as the
method has several elements that are not covered by the Proposition. The result
is of purely heuristic value. What makes it even more limited is that (C3)
basically assumes the central thing I'd be curious about, namely that the
"effective sample size" as defined in the given setting is the right one to use
(as is admitted in Remark 1). I accept the proof now but I have my doubts
whether the Proposition says much of interest (enough to justify the lengthy
treatment of it). The paper may just be better (shorter and easier to read)
without it. But I leave this decision to you (OK the detailed discussion is of
some value, but also long).
